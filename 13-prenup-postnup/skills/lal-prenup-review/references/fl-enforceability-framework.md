# Florida Prenup / Postnup Enforceability Framework

## PRENUPTIAL AGREEMENTS — §61.079

### What Makes a Prenup Unenforceable

§61.079(7)(a): A prenuptial agreement is not enforceable if the party
against whom enforcement is sought proves **either**:

**Ground 1 — Involuntariness:**
The agreement was the product of fraud, duress, coercion, or overreaching.

Key factors courts examine:
- Timing of first presentation vs. execution (less than 2 weeks = flag)
- Timing of execution vs. wedding date (within 30 days = significant flag;
  same day / day before = strong indicator)
- Whether the challenging party had independent legal counsel
- Whether the challenging party had adequate time to review and consult
- Whether any ultimatum was communicated (express or implied)
- Economic dependence of the challenging party on the drafting party
- Any language barrier, comprehension issue, or vulnerability

**Ground 2 — Inadequate Disclosure:**
Before execution, the party was not provided a fair and reasonable disclosure
of property or financial obligations of the other party **AND**:
- Did not voluntarily and expressly waive, in writing, any right to disclosure
  beyond what was provided; **AND**
- Did not have adequate knowledge of the property or financial obligations

*Note: If a valid written waiver of disclosure exists, Ground 2 is defeated
even if no schedules were provided. The waiver must be knowing and voluntary.*

### Alimony — Special Rule

§61.079(7)(b): Even if the agreement is otherwise enforceable, a court **may**
modify or eliminate any provision that would cause one party to be eligible
for public assistance at the time of divorce. This is not a full unenforceability
ground — it is a narrow, provision-specific relief available even when the
agreement otherwise holds.

### What Courts Cannot Do

A court cannot refuse to enforce a prenup simply because:
- Circumstances changed since execution
- The agreement turned out to be unfavorable for one party
- The parties reconciled after a separation

These are specifically not grounds for unenforceability under §61.079.

## POSTNUPTIAL AGREEMENTS — Contract Law + Case Law

### Governing Standard

Postnups are governed by Florida contract law, not §61.079.
Florida courts apply **heightened scrutiny** to postnups.

**Core requirements:**
1. Must be in writing and signed by both parties
2. Must have independent consideration beyond the continuation of the marriage
3. Must be fair and reasonable at time of execution
4. May be examined for fairness at time of enforcement in some contexts

### Consideration Requirement

The continuation of the marriage alone is NOT adequate consideration in Florida.
The agreement must identify specific, independent consideration:
- Transfer of property or assets
- Payment of money
- Satisfaction of a debt
- Relinquishment of a claim or legal right
- Contemporaneous financial benefit

Agreements without adequate consideration are voidable.

### Alimony in Postnups

Florida courts have struck alimony waivers in postnups with notable frequency,
particularly when:
- The waiving party is in a weaker financial position
- No independent counsel was present
- The waiver appears to have been induced by marital pressure or economic dependence
- No meaningful benefit was provided to the waiving party as consideration

*Practice note:* In Neave Family Law Group, P.A.'s experience, alimony waiver in a postnup is the
provision most likely to be challenged and most likely to succeed if challenged.
Always flag to attorney before including.

### Voluntariness in Postnups

Courts look at the totality of circumstances surrounding execution:
- Was the marriage in crisis when the postnup was signed?
- Was there a threat of divorce without adequate alternative remedy?
- Was the proposing party in a position of economic dominance?
- Did both parties have independent counsel?
- Was there adequate time for review?

### Independent Counsel

While not statutorily required, the absence of independent counsel for the
challenging party is a significant factor in postnup challenge cases. Neave Family Law Group, P.A.
should document each party's counsel status in every postnup.

## QUICK REFERENCE: Challenge Ground Strength Assessment

### Strong Challenge Indicators
- Signed day of or day before the wedding
- No independent counsel for challenging party
- No financial schedules and no valid written waiver of disclosure
- Significant wealth disparity with no countervailing protections
- Postnup: no adequate consideration identified
- Postnup: signed during period of severe marital conflict
- Prohibited provisions present (child support, parenting, morality)
- Execution defects (missing signatures, witnesses, notary)

### Moderate Challenge Indicators
- First presentation less than 2 weeks before execution
- One party's counsel was not independent (paid by the other party, etc.)
- Financial schedules attached but incomplete or understated
- Alimony waiver without meaningful disclosure support
- Postnup: one-sided benefits without balancing provision for other party

### Weak Challenge Indicators
- Agreement is financially unfavorable but was properly executed
- Circumstances changed since execution
- Party now regrets signing
- Agreement turns out to have been a bad deal
